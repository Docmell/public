Day 1 · Sat 10 Oct · Risk Management · id 1
Most accounts are not destroyed by one bad idea. They are destroyed by one oversized position.

The 1% rule is simple: decide in advance that no single trade can cost you more than about 1% of your trading capital. On a RM10,000 account, that is RM100. Not RM1,000 because you feel confident. RM100.

Why so small? Because losing streaks are normal, even for skilled traders. At 1% risk, ten losses in a row still leaves you with roughly 90% of your capital and a clear head. At 10% risk, the same streak leaves you with about a third of your account.

Small risk is not about being timid. It is about surviving long enough for your edge, your skill and your process to actually show up in the results.

Day 2 · Sun 11 Oct · Trading Psychology · id 11
The candle is huge, everyone online is talking about it, and you feel you have to get in right now. That feeling has a name: FOMO.

Fear of missing out pushes traders to enter late, after most of the move has already happened. The problem is not only the price. A late entry is often far from any sensible stop-loss level, so you either take a much bigger risk or place a stop that has no logic behind it.

Markets offer opportunities every week. Missing one move costs you nothing. Chasing a bad entry can cost you real money and confidence.

A practical fix: define your entry zone before the move happens. If price runs away without you, write it in your journal as a missed trade and move on. Patience is a skill you can practise.

Day 3 · Mon 12 Oct · Technical Analysis · id 25
Support and resistance are among the first concepts most traders learn, and for good reason. They help you see where the market has reacted before.

Support is an area where price has previously stopped falling, because buying interest was strong enough to absorb selling. Resistance is the opposite: an area where rising price has stalled, as selling interest outweighed buying.

A few practical tips. Draw zones, not razor-thin lines, because price rarely respects an exact number. Levels that have been tested several times, or that line up with round numbers, often get more attention. And remember that no level is permanent. When resistance breaks, it can later act as support, and vice versa.

Use these zones to plan, not predict: they are useful for placing stop-losses and setting realistic targets.

Day 4 · Tue 13 Oct · Position Sizing · id 19
Position size is not a guess and not a feeling. It is a simple calculation you can do before every trade.

Step 1: decide how much of your account you are willing to risk. On a RM5,000 account at 1%, that is RM50.

Step 2: find your stop distance. If you plan to enter at RM2.00 with a stop at RM1.90, you risk RM0.10 per share.

Step 3: divide. RM50 divided by RM0.10 equals 500 shares, or 5 lots on Bursa Malaysia, where one board lot is 100 shares. The position value is RM1,000, but your planned risk stays at RM50.

Notice what this does: a wider stop automatically gives a smaller position, and a tighter stop allows a larger one, while your risk in RM stays the same. Just leave a little room for brokerage fees and slippage.

If your rules only exist in your head, they will change the moment the market gets emotional.

A trading plan is a written rulebook you create before you trade. It does not need to be long, but it should answer clear questions: What do I trade? When do I trade? What exactly makes me enter? Where do I exit if I am wrong? Where do I take profit? How much do I risk?

Add a section for your personal limits too. For example: stop for the day after two losing trades, or no trading when tired or upset. These rules protect you from yourself on bad days.

Day 6 · Thu 15 Oct · Orders & Execution · id 41
Two buttons, two very different outcomes. Do you know which one you are pressing?

A market order says: get me in or out now. It prioritises speed, so you will almost always be filled, but the final price can differ from what you saw on screen, especially in fast or thin markets.

A limit order says: only at this price or better. It gives you control over price, but there is a chance the market never reaches your level and the order stays unfilled. You might miss a move entirely.

Neither is better in every situation. Many traders use limit orders for planned entries and accept market orders when exiting quickly matters more than a few cents.

Day 8 · Sat 17 Oct · Scam Awareness · id 52
If someone promises fixed, high returns from trading, that alone is a reason to walk away.

Financial markets are uncertain by nature. Even skilled professionals have losing months. So when an offer claims steady, high profits every week or month with no mention of risk, ask yourself where that money is really coming from. In many scams, early 'returns' are simply paid out of new investors' deposits until the scheme collapses.

Too-good-to-be-true offers often come with other warning signs: pressure to decide quickly, rewards for recruiting friends, vague explanations of the strategy, and difficulty withdrawing your money.

Before putting in a single ringgit, check whether the company and individuals are licensed by the Securities Commission Malaysia, and look them up on the SC Investor Alert List.

Day 9 · Sun 18 Oct · Market Fundamentals · id 57
Interest rates are often called the price of money, and that price touches almost every market.

Central banks adjust policy rates to manage inflation and support economic growth. In Malaysia, Bank Negara Malaysia's Monetary Policy Committee decides the Overnight Policy Rate (OPR). In the US, the Federal Reserve sets its own policy rate, which is closely watched worldwide.

When rates rise, borrowing tends to become more expensive, savings may earn more, and a currency can become more attractive to some investors. When rates fall, the opposite pressures can apply. But these are tendencies, not rules. Markets usually price in what they expect beforehand, so the reaction often depends on whether the decision or the guidance was a surprise.

That's why traders mark rate decisions on their calendar well in advance.

Day 11 · Tue 20 Oct · Trading Psychology · id 12
You just took a painful loss. Within minutes, you are in another trade, bigger than usual, with no real setup. Sound familiar?

That is revenge trading: trying to win back a loss immediately, driven by frustration rather than analysis. It feels like taking control, but it usually means abandoning the very rules that protect you.

The market is not personal. It does not know you lost, and it owes you nothing. Each trade has to meet your criteria on its own merits, regardless of what happened before.

Build a circuit breaker into your plan. After a loss that stings, step away for a fixed time. Walk, drink some water, write down what happened. Come back only when you can look at the chart calmly. Combined with a daily loss limit, this habit can save your account.

Day 12 · Wed 21 Oct · Technical Analysis · id 26
Before deciding how to trade a chart, it helps to answer a basic question: is the market trending or not?

The classic definition is simple. In an uptrend, price makes higher highs and higher lows. Each swing up goes further than the last, and each pullback stops above the previous low. In a downtrend, you see lower highs and lower lows.

When highs and lows overlap without a clear direction, the market is likely ranging. Strategies that work well in trends often struggle in ranges, so recognising this can save you from many frustrating trades.

A useful habit is to start on a higher timeframe. The trend on the daily chart gives context for what you see on the 1-hour chart. A trend is also only a description of what has happened so far. A break of the latest higher low is an early sign the structure may be changing.

Day 13 · Thu 22 Oct · Position Sizing · id 20
How you size your trades shapes your results as much as your entries do. Here are two common approaches.

Fixed lot sizing means trading the same size every time, for example 0.10 lot or 5 lots. It is easy to use, but the actual amount at risk changes depending on how far away your stop is. A wide stop can quietly mean several times the risk of a tight one. It also does not adjust as your account balance changes.

Fixed fractional sizing means risking the same percentage of your current account on every trade, such as 1%. When the account grows, the RM amount grows. During a drawdown, it shrinks automatically, which slows down losses when you need it most.

Many traders start with fixed lots for simplicity, then move to fixed fractional once they are comfortable calculating size.

Day 15 · Sat 24 Oct · Orders & Execution · id 42
Your stop-loss is your exit plan. But not every stop order works the same way.

A standard stop order (often called a stop-loss or stop market) is triggered when price reaches your stop level and then executes as a market order. You will very likely get out, but the fill price can be worse than your stop if the market gaps or moves very fast. This is called slippage.

A stop-limit order is triggered the same way, but turns into a limit order instead. That protects you from a bad fill price, yet if price jumps past your limit, the order may not execute at all and your loss can keep growing.

Order types and names vary between brokers and markets, so read your platform's documentation and test on demo first.

Day 16 · Sun 25 Oct · Common Mistakes · id 47
'It's cheaper now, so I'll buy more.' This thought has damaged many trading accounts.

Averaging down means adding to a position that is already losing to lower your average price. It feels smart because you need a smaller bounce to break even. But it also increases your exposure exactly when the market is telling you your idea may be wrong.

In the example on the slide, a single 100-unit position falling to RM1.20 would lose RM80. After averaging down, the same fall loses RM120. If the decline continues, the gap keeps growing.

Some long-term investors deliberately add to positions as part of a pre-set plan with fixed amounts. That is very different from a trader adding in panic with no stop. Know which one you are doing.

Day 18 · Tue 27 Oct · Market Fundamentals · id 58
Your balance can stay the same while what it buys gets smaller.

Inflation is the general increase in the prices of goods and services. In Malaysia, it is measured through the Consumer Price Index (CPI), published by the Department of Statistics Malaysia. When inflation rises, each ringgit buys a little less than before. That loss of purchasing power is easy to overlook because it happens slowly.

This is why it helps to think in real terms. If your savings grow by 2% while prices rise by 3%, you are roughly 1% worse off in purchasing power, even though the number in your account went up.

Inflation also matters to markets: it influences central bank interest rate decisions, which can affect currencies, bonds and stocks. That's why CPI releases are major events on the economic calendar.

Day 19 · Wed 28 Oct · Risk Management · id 3
Two traders can win the same number of trades and end up with very different results. Risk-reward is often the reason.

The risk-reward ratio compares how much you stand to lose if your stop is hit with how much you aim to make if your target is reached. Risking RM50 to aim for RM100 is 1:2.

This matters because it changes the win rate you need. At 1:1, you need to win more than half your trades just to break even. At 1:2, roughly one win in three covers two losses, before fees and spreads.

A word of caution: a big ratio on paper means nothing if the target is unrealistic. Set targets at sensible levels such as the next resistance, then check whether the ratio is worth taking. Sometimes the best trade is no trade.

Day 20 · Thu 29 Oct · Trading Psychology · id 13
Five wins in a row. You feel unstoppable. This is exactly when many traders give back weeks of progress.

Winning streaks can quietly change behaviour. Position sizes creep up, entry criteria become looser, and stop-losses feel less necessary. One oversized loss can then erase several good trades.

It helps to remember that streaks happen naturally, in both directions. Market conditions may simply suit your strategy for a while. That does not mean your edge has suddenly doubled.

The disciplined response is boring but effective: keep your risk per trade unchanged, stick to the same checklist, and review your winners as carefully as your losers. If you want to increase size, do it gradually, based on a larger account balance and a solid sample of trades, not on a feeling.

Day 21 · Fri 30 Oct · Technical Analysis · id 27
Every candlestick tells a short story about what buyers and sellers did during one period of time.

Each candle records four prices: open, high, low and close. The thick part, called the body, runs from the open to the close. If the close is higher than the open, it is a bullish candle, often shown in green or white. If the close is lower, it is bearish, often red or black.

The thin lines above and below the body are the wicks, or shadows. They show the highest and lowest prices reached. A long upper wick means price rose but was pushed back down before the close. A long lower wick means sellers pushed price down but buyers brought it back up.

One candle on its own says little. Read candles in context: where they appear relative to trend, support and resistance matters much more.

Day 22 · Sat 31 Oct · Position Sizing · id 21
You do not have to enter or exit a position all at once. Scaling lets you do it in stages, but it needs rules.

Scaling in means building a position in parts. For example, if your total planned risk is 1%, you might enter half at your first level and add the second half only if price confirms your idea. The key is that the combined risk of all parts never exceeds your plan.

Scaling out means closing a position in parts, such as taking half off at a first target and letting the rest run with a trailing stop. This can reduce the stress of deciding on a single exit.

The big pitfall is adding to a losing trade to average down without a plan. It increases your exposure exactly when the market is telling you that you might be wrong.

Would you rather discover a strategy's weakness on old charts, or with your own savings?

Backtesting means applying your trading rules to historical price data to see how they would have performed. You can do it manually by scrolling back on a chart, or with software if you code.

A few honest habits make backtests more useful. Avoid hindsight: only use information that was visible at that moment. Include trending, ranging and volatile periods, not just one type of market. A backtest cannot tell you what will happen next. It can, however, show you whether an idea is worth testing further, and prepare you for the drawdowns it may bring.

Day 24 · Mon 2 Nov · Orders & Execution · id 43
Ever opened a trade and instantly seen a small loss? That is the spread at work.

Every market has two prices: the bid (what buyers will pay) and the ask (what sellers want). If you buy at the ask and immediately sell at the bid, you lose the difference. That gap is a cost you pay on every trade.

Slippage is different. It happens when your order fills at a different price than you expected, often because price moved between clicking and execution, or because there was not enough volume at your price. It can occasionally work in your favour, but in fast markets it often does not.

For short-term traders taking many small trades, spread and slippage can make the difference between a strategy that works on paper and one that does not.

Day 28 · Fri 6 Nov · Risk Management · id 4
"I win 70% of my trades!" sounds impressive. But it tells you almost nothing on its own.

What matters is expectancy: the average amount you make or lose per trade over many trades. It combines how often you win with how big your wins and losses are.

A trader who wins 70% of the time but takes small profits and lets losses run can still lose money overall. In our example, that works out to minus RM10 per trade. Meanwhile, a trader who wins only 40% of the time but keeps losses small and lets winners run comes out ahead.

The lesson: stop chasing a high win rate for its own sake. Keep a trading journal, record every result, and calculate your expectancy honestly. Your numbers will tell you far more than your feelings.

Day 29 · Sat 7 Nov · Trading Psychology · id 14
Small wins, big losses. If that describes your results, loss aversion may be part of the story.

Behavioural finance research has found that, for many people, the pain of losing feels roughly twice as strong as the pleasure of gaining the same amount. In trading, this shows up in two ways.

First, we hold losing trades too long, because closing them makes the loss feel real. Second, we close winning trades too early, because we are afraid the profit will disappear. Over time, this produces small winners and large losers, which is a tough combination to overcome.

The fix is to make exit decisions before emotions get involved. Set your stop-loss and your target, or your trailing rule, when you enter. Then track in your journal how often you follow the plan versus override it.

Day 30 · Sun 8 Nov · Technical Analysis · id 28
Moving averages are one of the most widely used indicators, and one of the most misunderstood.

A moving average smooths price data by averaging a set number of past prices. A 50-period simple moving average, for example, adds up the last 50 closes and divides by 50. The result is a line that makes the general direction of price easier to see.

The trade-off is lag. Because a moving average is calculated from past prices, it always reacts after price has already moved. A longer average, like 200 periods, is smoother but slower. A shorter one, like 20 periods, reacts faster but is noisier. Exponential moving averages give more weight to recent prices, which reduces lag a little but does not remove it.

Moving averages work best as a guide to trend context, not as a crystal ball. In choppy markets, expect many false crossovers.

Day 37 · Sun 15 Nov · Risk Management · id 5

Day 39 · Tue 17 Nov · Technical Analysis · id 29
RSI above 70, so the market must turn down now, right? Not necessarily.

The Relative Strength Index, developed by J. Welles Wilder, compares the size of recent up moves with recent down moves and plots the result between 0 and 100. The default setting is usually 14 periods. Readings above 70 are traditionally called overbought, and readings below 30 oversold.

The common mistake is treating those levels as automatic reversal signals. In a strong uptrend, RSI can stay above 70 for weeks while price keeps rising. Overbought describes strong momentum. It does not mean price has to fall.

RSI tends to be more useful as supporting evidence: checking whether momentum agrees with the trend, spotting divergences between price and RSI, or confirming a reaction at a support or resistance zone.

Day 40 · Wed 18 Nov · Position Sizing · id 23
A margin call is your broker telling you that your account no longer has enough equity to safely support your open positions.

When you trade on margin, part of your balance is set aside as used margin. Your margin level compares your equity, which includes open profits and losses, to that used margin. With RM2,000 equity and RM1,000 used margin, your margin level is 200%.

As losses grow, equity falls and so does the margin level. Many brokers send a margin call around a set level, often 100%, and have a lower stop-out level, for example 50%, where they start closing positions automatically, usually beginning with the biggest loser. Exact levels differ by broker, so check yours.

The best protection is simple: use modest position sizes, keep used margin low relative to your equity, and always use stop-losses.

Day 45 · Mon 23 Nov · Risk Management · id 6
Leverage is often marketed as a way to make more from a small account. What is mentioned less often: it lets you lose more, just as fast.

With leverage, you only put up a fraction of the position value as margin. At 1:100, RM1,000 can control a RM100,000 position. That means a 1% price move equals RM1,000 in profit or loss. A move that small can wipe out the full margin.

Leverage itself is a tool, not the enemy. The danger comes from using all of it. The fact that your broker allows 1:500 does not mean your position should be anywhere near that size.
